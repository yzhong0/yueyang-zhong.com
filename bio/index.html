Bio

Yueyang Zhong is an Assistant Professor of Management Science and Operations at London Business School. She studies human-centered operations: how people behave in congested service systems, how that behavior can be learned from data, and how to design services where delay has real consequences, from hospitals to ride-hailing platforms. Before joining LBS in 2024 she was a Postdoctoral Principal Researcher at Yale School of Management and Chicago Booth, hosted by Vahideh Manshadi and Rad Niazadeh. She received her PhD in Operations Management from Chicago Booth in 2023.

Research

Her research runs in four directions. Queueing theory is the analytical engine; strategic behavior, imperfect information and real stakes are the recurring themes.

Behavioral and strategic queueing

She investigates strategic behavior in queueing systems: how the environment shapes what customers and servers do, how the two sides interact, and what follows for staffing, pay, admission and design. The cases are concrete: servers who rebel against overload, miners racing for a winner-takes-all reward, platforms choosing between surge pricing and a virtual queue. Because such systems resist exact analysis, she relies on fluid and diffusion approximations in many-server regimes, alongside empirical and experimental studies.

Learning in queues

She uses structural results from queueing theory to design online learning algorithms that decide well while a system's parameters are unknown: which customer class to serve next when service and patience rates must be estimated, how to dispatch ambulances efficiently and fairly by learning from past calls, and how to learn policies from platform experiments that cannot all be trusted.

High-stakes operations in congested systems

She studies the interplay of efficiency, quality and equity in healthcare and other congested public services: how a maternity unit should ration operating-theatre capacity, how deprivation and congestion shape maternal and neonatal outcomes, how primary care trades continuity against access, and how childcare centers manage year-long waiting lists. Combining stochastic modeling, machine learning and empirical methods, she builds operational solutions that allocate scarce resources equitably and improve patient outcomes.

Information and mechanism design

She designs information and incentives for settings where mistakes are costly and the designer's own knowledge is incomplete. One strand asks what people should be told: nudges that keep mental-health clients in therapy, dynamic information that sustains engagement, and firms' AI-risk disclosures. The other asks how to regulate emerging technologies that nobody, the regulator included, understands well: robust mechanisms that manage the risks and rewards of AI development for positive, sustainable societal impact. Both strands pair theoretical modeling with empirical analysis and field experiments.
